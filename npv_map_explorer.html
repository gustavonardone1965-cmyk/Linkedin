Where does an investment stop creating value?

A project's value depends on many things, but two usually dominate: the rate investors demand and the price the product sells for. This map shows every combination of both at once, and the frontier where the project stops creating value. Move the sliders and watch it change.

How to read it. Each point is one combination of two variables: the cost of equity (horizontal axis, 0% to 60% per year: risk-free rate + country risk + project premium) and the selling price (vertical axis, −50% to +50% versus the base price). The colour is the project's NPV in that combination, in today's dollars over the whole project life: green creates value, pink destroys it; the stronger the colour, the larger the amount. The black line is the frontier where NPV is exactly zero. The circle “Today” is where the sliders place the project. Each slider has a box next to it to type the exact figure of a real case, including values beyond the slider range. Hover or tap the map to read any combination.

What the map says right now

    Why the frontier tilts

    A higher rate makes future cash worth less today, so the project needs a higher price to stay at zero NPV. The slope of the black line is that trade-off: how much price compensates each extra point of rate.

    Why IRR alone is not enough

    The IRR depends only on the cash flows, so it does not move with the rate. It tells you where the frontier crosses at a given price, but the map shows how far you are from it in both directions at once.

    How to use it

    Look at the distance from “Today” to the black line. A short distance means a fragile project: secure the price (contracts) or the rate (fixed financing) before investing. A long distance means room for bad news.

    About this model: a learning tool, not a valuation

    This explorer is a conceptual learning format. Its purpose is to build intuition about how the cost of capital and the selling price interact to create or destroy value, and to make the zero-NPV frontier visible. It is deliberately simple so that every result can be traced back to a few assumptions. Use it to understand, explain and frame a decision, not to make one on its own.

    The concept in one paragraph

    A project creates value when its future cash flows, discounted at the return investors require, exceed what it costs today. That required return rises with the risk-free rate, country risk and the project's own risk, and falls as cheaper debt is added (the WACC). The selling price drives the cash flows. Every combination of rate and price gives one NPV; the line where NPV equals zero separates the projects worth doing from those that are not. The distance from where you stand today to that line is your margin of safety.

    What the model assumes

    • Constant cash flows: the same revenue and costs every year, with no ramp-up, growth, seasonality or inflation.
    • One investment at the start: no expansions, overhauls or replacements during the project life.
    • Straight-line depreciation of the full investment over the project life.
    • Tax on every year's result, even when negative: it assumes the company can offset project losses against other profits. No loss carry-forwards, no tax holidays.
    • Price moves alone: a price change affects revenue but not volume; variable costs stay at their base-price level.
    • Working capital as a share of revenue, invested at the start and fully recovered at the end; salvage value taxed in full in the last year.
    • Constant financing mix: the WACC uses fixed weights of equity and debt. It does not model loan schedules, grace periods or the investor's cash flow.
    • A single currency (USD) with no exchange-rate effects; no indirect taxes (VAT) or their timing.

    How to use it with judgment

    • Read distances, not points. The exact NPV matters less than how far “Today” is from the frontier, in both price and rate.
    • Stress the drivers you are least sure of. If the price or country risk is uncertain, move it to a pessimistic value and check which side of the line you land on.
    • Use the trade-off. The price-per-rate-point figure tells you how much pricing power you need to absorb a rise in rates.
    • Check the inputs make sense together. A high price with the same volume, or a long life with no reinvestment, can flatter the result.
    • Move to a full model before deciding. Real projects need year-by-year cash flows: ramp-up, product mix, loan schedules, taxes, working capital timing and a risk analysis such as Monte Carlo simulation.
